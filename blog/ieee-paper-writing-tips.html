Research Writing

IEEE Access Paper Writing Tips

A practical structure for engineers who want to turn strong technical work into a clear, reviewable manuscript.

By Dr. Musaad AlruwailiUpdated July 27, 20267 min readIEEE

Contents

    Start with a precise problem

    A strong paper does not begin with a broad topic. It begins with a specific engineering problem, a clear gap in existing work, and a contribution that can be tested or demonstrated. For example, "AI in wireless communication" is a topic. "A secure matching and resource allocation method for 5G device-to-device communication under interference constraints" is closer to a research problem.

    Before writing the introduction, write three private sentences for yourself: what problem exists, why current methods are not enough, and what your paper contributes. If those sentences are vague, the paper will usually become vague too. If they are precise, every section becomes easier to organize.

    Build the manuscript around evidence

    • Explain the system model or experimental setup clearly.
    • Use figures that tell the story without overloading the reader.
    • Compare against meaningful baselines, not only your own previous result.
    • Make limitations visible so reviewers trust the work.
    • Write the abstract after the results are stable.

    Evidence is the center of an engineering manuscript. A method section should make the work reproducible enough for another researcher to understand the system model, assumptions, parameters, and evaluation setup. A results section should not only show that the proposed method performs well; it should explain why the results matter under realistic constraints.

    Structure the introduction carefully

    A useful introduction moves from context to gap to contribution. Start with the engineering problem and its importance. Then summarize what existing studies have achieved and where they remain limited. After that, state the contribution in direct language. Avoid making the reader hunt for novelty.

    Contribution bullets should be specific. Instead of writing "This paper improves performance," explain the mechanism: secure matching, adaptive key generation, interference-aware power allocation, reduced outage probability, lower computation cost, or stronger privacy protection. Reviewers are more receptive when the contribution can be tested against a clear claim.

    Figures should carry the argument

    Good figures reduce reviewer effort. A system model figure can show network entities, data flow, control decisions, and assumptions. A flowchart can explain an algorithm more clearly than several paragraphs. Results figures should have readable labels, units, legends, and captions that explain the takeaway.

    Before submission, print each figure or view it at a small size. If a reader cannot understand the axes, units, or comparison groups quickly, the figure needs work. A strong paper is not only mathematically correct; it is visually understandable.

    Reviewer mindset

    Reviewers look for novelty, clarity, validation, and relevance. A good response letter answers each comment respectfully, points to exact revisions, and improves the paper instead of only defending it. When a reviewer asks for clarification, assume the manuscript did not make the point visible enough. The revision should help the next reader, not only satisfy the current reviewer.

    For each comment, use a simple pattern: thank the reviewer, state what was changed, mention where the change appears, and explain any technical reasoning. If you disagree with a comment, do so respectfully and support the response with evidence from the model, experiment, or literature.
